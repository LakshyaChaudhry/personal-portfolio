*Lakshya Chaudhry, Purdue University.*

Reinforcement learning with a checker has become a default way to make language models better at reasoning. The usual recipe is GRPO: sample several answers to the same problem, score them, and nudge the model toward the ones that scored higher than the rest of the group.

There is a simpler thing you can do with the same checker. Sample answers, throw away the wrong ones, and fine-tune on the ones that are right. That is rejection-sampling fine-tuning, RFT.

These two are hard to compare, because GRPO is also a data filter. If all eight samples for a prompt are right, or all eight are wrong, that prompt gives GRPO nothing to learn from. It quietly trains on the problems in the middle. Recent work finds that GRPO learns more from mixed-difficulty data, and some of that work treats the filtering as the real reason RL beats supervised fine-tuning.

If that story is right, a supervised method given the same data should catch up.

I tested that. It mostly does not.

---

## The setup

The task is procedural counting, from Bauer et al. (2026). A problem starts with a range of integers, applies a few filters and transformations, and asks for one number back: a count, a sum, a median. A short program computes the answer, so every completion can be checked exactly. No fuzzy grading.

The model is Qwen3-4B-Base, trained with LoRA. I did not search hyperparameters for GRPO. It runs one recipe taken from Bauer et al., with two departures: the reward is only whether the final answer is right, and each step uses 8 prompts (64 completions). RFT got a 27-run sweep over learning rate and epochs, and I kept the setting with the best validation accuracy. If GRPO still wins, it is not because the baseline was left untuned.

Six main arms cross the two methods with three prompt sets, three seeds each. The predictions and the rule for what would count as an effect were written down before any training run.

The prompt sets:

- **Easy.** 100 problems the base model already solves most of the time (at least 6 of 8 samples correct).
- **Mixed.** 100 problems, about a third easy, a third medium, a third hard. Difficulty is this model's own pass rate, not a panel of other models.
- **Curated.** The 73 mixed problems where the base model was sometimes right and sometimes wrong (1 to 7 correct out of 8). Those are the prompts that give a GRPO group of eight a learning signal. The set is chosen once and then frozen.

The easy and mixed arms also match on rollouts: 19,200 sampled completions each. RFT draws them once, up front, from the base model. GRPO redraws them from the current model over 300 steps. I did not equalize training tokens or optimizer steps. That difference is the thing being compared. GRPO learns from wrong answers, and from how an answer ranks inside its group. RFT only ever sees successes.

Every run uses the same 4,352-token cap. A completion that hits the cap scores zero, in training and at evaluation.

The number I report is greedy accuracy on 300 held-out problems, 100 per difficulty tier.

## What happened

| Arm | Test accuracy | Cut off at the token cap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Base model | 67.3% | 4.3% |
| RFT, easy | 65.9% ± 1.1 | 2.4% |
| RFT, mixed | 69.6% ± 1.0 | 2.0% |
| RFT, curated | 66.9% ± 1.1 | 3.1% |
| GRPO, easy | 74.3% ± 3.9 | 3.0% |
| GRPO, mixed | 78.8% ± 0.5 | 8.7% |
| GRPO, curated | 77.8% ± 1.7 | 7.6% |
| Iterated RFT, curated | 70.1% ± 0.5 | 1.6% |

Means over three seeds. The base model is a single run. GRPO on mixed and curated prompts is past a 5% truncation flag I set before training.

**GRPO wins on every prompt set.** On the curated prompts it leads by 10.9 points (77.8% vs. 66.9%). All three seed pairs agree, by 12.0, 10.3, and 10.3 points. On the easy and mixed prompts, where the number of rollouts matches, it leads by 8.4 and 9.2 points. I added those two comparisons after seeing the test results, so they are post hoc. They say the same thing: the extra rollouts GRPO receives on the curated set are not what creates the gap.

On the test set the lead survives the reading that is most unfair to GRPO. Count every cut-off RFT answer as correct and every cut-off GRPO answer as wrong, and GRPO still leads by 6.0 to 7.8 points. Out of distribution, 20–55% of GRPO completions hit the cap. That is enough to flip the comparison, so I do not conclude anything there.

**The gain is on hard problems.** The base model is already at 92% on the easy tier, and all six main arms stay between 92% and 97%. On the 100 hard test problems the base model scores 29.0%. The RFT arms score 23.3–29.7%. The GRPO arms score 40.7% (easy prompts), 58.0% (mixed), and 52.7% (curated). Those GRPO numbers are lower bounds, because the hard tier is also where GRPO hits the token cap most often.

**Curating the prompts does not help RFT.** I had predicted that training RFT only on the prompts a GRPO group can learn from would recover most of the gap. It did not. RFT on the curated set scores 2.7 points below RFT on the full mixed set, and all three seed pairs point the same way. I do not treat that as evidence that curation hurts. A 2.7 point gap is about the size of the change I see when the same run is scored again on a different machine, up to 2.3 points. Curation does not help.

For GRPO, making the filter explicit changes nothing detectable. The curated arm is within 1.0 point of the mixed arm. That fits the filtering account. GRPO already gets no gradient from a group whose eight rewards all agree, and on the mixed prompts the share of those groups rises from about 26–29% early in training to 61–70% at the end. It filters its own prompts. Filtering them in advance adds little.

**Mixed-difficulty data helps RFT. For GRPO the evidence is inconclusive.** Mixed prompts beat easy prompts by 3.7 points for RFT, with the same sign out of distribution. That clears the bar I set in advance. GRPO's test gap is a similar 4.4 points, but the easy-prompt seeds range from 70.0% to 77.7%, and the out-of-distribution sign flips. The mixed-data effect Bauer et al. reported for RL shows up here for fine-tuning on correct samples. I cannot confirm it for GRPO, in part because the mixed GRPO runs truncate too often out of distribution.

**Sampling from the current model recovers about 30% of the gap.** Single-round RFT learns only from the base model's samples. GRPO samples from the model as it changes, every step. A secondary arm runs three rounds of RFT on the curated prompts, and rounds two and three sample from the model the previous round just trained. It gains 3.2 points on single-round RFT. GRPO still leads that arm by 7.7. Added together, 3.2 and 7.7 are the original 10.9. About 30% of the gap moves when the samples get fresher. About 70% stays with everything this arm still does not do: learning from failed samples, comparing samples inside a group, and refreshing the policy 300 times instead of 3.

Two limits on that 30%. The iterated arm also takes more optimizer steps and trains on more samples, so it is not a pure "fresher data" effect. And 3.2 points is close to that 2.3 point machine-to-machine swing.

**GRPO makes a single try more reliable. It does not expand what the model can solve.** Per-sample accuracy (mean@8) rises from 42.5% for the base model to 70.1–77.1% after GRPO. Given 64 attempts on the first 100 test problems, the base model already solves 98.0%, and the GRPO arms solve 95.7–96.7%. Problems the base model gets occasionally become problems it gets reliably. The set of problems it can ever solve does not grow. That matches other reports that this kind of RL sharpens a distribution instead of widening it.

**A reward that ignores the answer does not reproduce the gain.** Two controls run the mixed-prompt GRPO recipe and change only the score. On the test set, correctness reaches 78.7% on that seed, a reward for merely writing down an answer scores 63.7%, and a random reward scores 47.7%. The base model is at 67.3%. Each control is one seed. The format reward does push sampled accuracy from 42.5% to 60.3% and the rate of explicit answer lines to 99%, so a bump in sampled accuracy can come from formatting alone.

Single-round RFT, with the best of nine configurations, does not improve greedy accuracy over the base model at this budget. RFT-Easy is 1.4 points below it. The other two arms have intervals that include zero. GRPO does move greedy accuracy.

## What I got wrong

I wrote down, before training, that curation would recover most of GRPO's advantage over RFT, and that the rest would be a modest leftover from negative samples, relative rewards, and training on the model's own latest samples. The leftover is most of the gap. None of the data-side changes I gave RFT recovered it: not the curated prompt set, not mixed difficulty, not three rounds of fresher samples.

That is a narrower claim than "reinforcement learning is doing something supervised learning cannot." Three features of this RFT could be leaving accuracy on the table.

It keeps every correct sample, with no cap per prompt, so the training set follows the base model's pass rate and is mostly easy problems. In the mixed split, 595 of 7,961 kept samples come from hard prompts, which is where GRPO's gain actually is. GRPO draws eight completions for every prompt it samples.

It samples once from the base model. Filtered methods that have matched GRPO in other papers resample from the current policy on every iteration. Three refreshes is not every step, and I did not run that experiment.

And the gap bundles several differences at once. Failed samples, a group-relative reward, 300 policy updates rather than one or three, a larger learning rate, and, on the curated set, more rollouts. Iterated RFT separates out part of the refresh. I did not separate the rest.

---

## What this is not

One synthetic task family. One base model. LoRA, not full fine-tuning. Three seeds on the main arms. Bauer et al. studied counting, graph reasoning, and spatial reasoning. I ran counting. The fixed budget went to a tuned baseline, three seeds, and two controls on one task, rather than a second task or a second model.

The success rule compares a difference to the spread across seeds and checks that the sign matches out of distribution. It is not a formal significance test. The three RFT seeds share one draw from the base model, so their spread misses the variance from sampling. Re-scoring a model on another machine moves test accuracy by up to 2.3 points. The smaller effects sit near that line. The 8–11 point lead on the test set does not.

These are measurements of two methods under fixed budgets. They are not a general result about reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards.
